# Recommendations for Making Ferrestan (Lanôstoje) More Naturalistic

## Concrete Edits, Organized by System

---

## 1. Break the Symmetry of the Article Vowel Grid

**The problem:** The o/e/a/i grid is too clean. No natural language maps four semantic categories onto four vowels in a perfect matrix.

**What to do:** Imagine this system had a history. At some earlier stage there were probably only two particles — maybe *o* for definite and *e* for indefinite, with no gender distinction. The animate/inanimate split developed later, and the inanimate forms *a* and *i* were derived from the original forms through some historical process. Let the traces of that history show:

- **Merge one or two cells.** Perhaps the inanimate indefinite (*i*) was historically identical to the animate indefinite (*e*) and only became distinct in certain cases. You could have *i* in nominative and accusative but *e* in dative and genitive for inanimate indefinite, with speakers disagreeing about which is "correct." This is the kind of partial split real languages show all the time.
- **Let one form be longer than the others.** Maybe the inanimate definite is actually *at* or *aj* rather than bare *a*, because it derives from an older demonstrative that only partially reduced. The grid would then look like *o / e / at / i* — still systematic, but asymmetric.
- **Introduce syncretism that doesn't follow the logic.** Right now definite-singular-animate nominative and accusative are both *os*, which is reasonable. But add some "unreasonable" syncretism too: maybe the inanimate definite dative and genitive collapse into the same form (*ad* for both) even though they are distinguished everywhere else. Real languages are full of these unmotivated mergers.

## 2. Make the Verb Modal Prefixes Messier

**The problem:** Single-consonant prefixes running through the alphabet, one meaning each. This is the most "designed-looking" part of the language.

**What to do:**

- **Give most modals 2–3 syllables, not 1 consonant.** In real languages, modals derive from full verbs via grammaticalization. English *must* < Old English *moste* (past tense of "may"). Japanese *-nakereba naranai* "must" is a whole clause that got fused. Even in heavily grammaticalized systems like Turkish, modal suffixes are at least a syllable (*-ebil-*, *-meli-*, *-ecek-*). So instead of *d-* for "must," maybe it's *dor-* or *dori-* (from a verb meaning "be obligated"). Instead of *h-* for "should," maybe *hef-* or *ahef-* (from a verb meaning "be fitting").
- **Collapse some distinctions.** "Want to" (*b-*) and "wish to" (*t-*) are near-synonyms. Real languages typically have one modal form covering this semantic space, with context disambiguating. Merge them into a single form, or make one of them an archaic/literary form that only appears in certain expressions.
- **Let some prefixes be polysemous.** Real modals are famously ambiguous. English *can* means both "is able to" and "has permission to." *Must* means both "is obligated to" and "I infer that." Instead of having *k-* mean only "could," let it mean both "is able to" and "has permission to" — the kind of overlap that drives language learners crazy but is perfectly natural.
- **Have one or two modals that work completely differently from the others.** Maybe the negation isn't a prefix at all in casual speech — maybe there's an alternate negative construction using a full word placed after the verb particle, coexisting with the prefix. Maybe the "certainly" marker *km-* is actually always a separate word that sits between the verb particle and the verb, not a prefix at all. Breaking the uniformity of the system makes it look less designed.

## 3. Introduce Sound Change Residue into Derivations

**The problem:** Every derived word is transparently analyzable. *arkê* (sad) → *arkêl* (cry) → *arkêsja* (anguish) — no phonological erosion anywhere.

**What to do:** Pick some derivational families and apply a historical sound change that has already partially happened. For example, if there was a vowel reduction rule where unstressed *e* → *ø* (dropped) in three-syllable words:

- *arkêsja* could have become something like *arkêsja* ~ *arksja* with the reduced form being more common in casual speech. Now it's no longer instantly parseable as *arkê* + *sja*.
- *trebfolo* ("abuse of power," from *tjabre* "authority" + *bfolo* "crime") already shows some morphological reshuffling — good! But push it further. Maybe the form used to be *tjabrebfolo* but contracted over time to *trebfolo* because the heavy cluster was simplified. Now the etymology is partially hidden, which is realistic.

Some specific targets:

- **The caf → cafte → caftê → ancêft chain** is too beautifully transparent. What if *ancêft* was actually *acêft* in standard speech (the *n* dropped in the cluster), and the *n*-form was considered archaic or regional? Or what if the word for "king" came from a completely different root in some clans, having been borrowed from the Dzemali or Kose Karti, and coexists with *ancêft*?
- **Body-part compounds.** *kmatsum* (center, from *kmat* "belly" + *sum*) and *sokmat* (guts, related to *kmat*) already show some reshuffling — *sokmat* reversed the order. Do more of this. Maybe *brenki* (shoulder) was once *-enki* (arm) + some prefix, but the prefix has eroded to *br-* and the connection to "arm" is no longer obvious to speakers.

## 4. Add Real Irregularity to the Paradigms

**The problem:** The article and verb particle paradigms are too regular.

**What to do:**

- **Irregular plural for the most common article combinations.** The definite animate plural goes *o* → *au*, which is regular (diphthongization). But what if the most frequently used case form — say, the definite animate plural accusative — had an irregular form that resisted the pattern? Maybe instead of the expected *aus*, it's actually *aw* or *â* in everyday speech, because it's used so often that it got phonologically reduced. High-frequency forms are always the most irregular in natural languages.
- **Give a few very common verbs suppletive particles or irregular stems.** "To be," "to go," "to come," and "to do/make" are irregular in almost every language on earth. Perhaps the verb *bije* (walk) or *cel* (to move from place to place) uses a different verb particle form — maybe *uf cel* (future continuous of "move") is actually *ul cel* because of some old irregular pattern. Or the perfective of *face* (die) isn't *fu face* but *fac* (the verb and particle fuse into a single word) because death is spoken of differently.
- **Add gaps.** Maybe the comitative case of the inanimate article simply doesn't exist — speakers use a periphrastic construction with a full word instead. Natural paradigms have holes.

---

## 5. Let the Prepositional Prefixes Show Merger and Polysemy

**The problem:** 50+ prefixes, each with one meaning. Real prepositions are always polysemous and overlapping.

**What to do:**

- **Let one prefix cover multiple unrelated meanings.** You already have *l-* mapped to three meanings: "at," "beside," and "off." Good — that's realistic! But the document lists these as separate entries. In a real description, you'd present *l-* as a single prefix with a basic spatial sense whose specific meaning depends on context. Do this with more prefixes.
- **Show that some prefixes have replaced others historically.** Maybe *kt-* (in/within) has been expanding its territory at the expense of *daj-* (into), so that younger speakers use *kt-* for both "in" and "into," while older speakers maintain the distinction. This is exactly how prepositional systems evolve in real languages (cf. English "in" absorbing some uses of "within," or French "dans" absorbing "en").
- **Reduce the total count.** 50+ is a lot of bound prepositional prefixes. Many of the more specific ones (*numind-* "via, by way of"; *hbaj-* "during"; *sûm-* "by, passing by") feel like they should be independent adverbs or postpositions rather than bound prefixes. In a real language, the bound prefix system would contain maybe 15–25 of the most frequent and basic spatial/grammatical relations, and the rest would be expressed periphrastically or with combinations. Having a few that can optionally appear either as bound prefixes or as independent words (with some speakers using one form and others the other) would add realistic variability.

## 6. Add Lexical Irregularity and Opacity

**The problem:** The vocabulary is too semantically transparent and too evenly distributed across categories.

**What to do:**

- **Create some body-part terms with no analyzable etymology.** Right now *rilbo* (ear) and *bilbo* (nose) are suspiciously similar — they look like they share a root. If that's intentional, fine, but then explain why. If not, change one of them to something phonologically unrelated. Real body-part terms are typically among the oldest, most stable, and most opaque items in the lexicon.
- **Have some words mean multiple unrelated things.** *kro* already means both "three" and "rope for mooring a boat." That's a great polysemous collision — the kind that happens when historically unrelated roots merge through sound change. Do more of this. Perhaps *se* (rock/stone) is also a verb meaning "to remain" or "to insist," with speakers disagreeing about whether the meanings are related. Perhaps *nar* (sun) is also slang for something unrelated in one region.
- **Add some words that defy the derivational patterns.** The adjective suffix *-ne* is very productive (*utone* "windy," *dohne* "crooked"). But there should be some adjectives that look like they should use *-ne* but don't, and some words that end in *-ne* but aren't adjectives. English has the suffix *-ly* for adverbs, but *friendly*, *lovely*, and *lonely* are adjectives — that kind of category leak is missing from Ferrestan.
- **Include a few words with unexplained structure.** The word *smaroktwa* (to assist, close companion) is beautifully long and unanalyzable. More words like this — long, opaque, apparently not compound — would balance the very transparent compounds.

## 7. Introduce Dialectal and Sociolectal Variation

**The problem:** The language is presented as completely uniform.

**What to do:** This is perhaps the single most impactful change. Even small touches create enormous verisimilitude.

- **Phonological variation.** Maybe the /ɾ/~/ɹ/~/r/ variation for ⟨r⟩ isn't free — maybe coastal Ferrestans use /ɹ/ and mountain Ferrestans use /r/, and the Almandu speakers trill heavily. The recorder already noted the variation but attributed it to free alternation; reclassifying it as regional makes the language feel inhabited.
- **Lexical variation.** The document already notes *arna* vs. *rana* as a regional variant for "chief." Expand this: maybe coastal clans have a different word for "ship" borrowed from the Dzemali, while inland clans use *durbu*. Maybe there are two words for "bread" — one used in the north and one in the south. This costs nothing and adds a great deal of realism.
- **Generational variation.** Perhaps younger speakers have started using some prepositional prefixes as independent prepositions (detaching them from the article), and older speakers consider this sloppy. Or younger speakers are collapsing the definite/indefinite distinction for inanimate nouns in casual speech. This kind of change-in-progress is the lifeblood of real languages.
- **Register/style variation.** Perhaps the story-telling formula *"Numindaust asintê, jom u dest"* uses an archaic form of the verb particle or an old pronoun form that isn't used in everyday speech. Ritual language almost always preserves archaic forms.

## 8. Add Exceptions and Oddities to the Circumflex System

**The problem:** Every consonant has exactly one circumflex realization, completely specified.

**What to do:**

- **Leave some combinations unattested.** Perhaps ⟨w⟩ before a circumflex vowel simply does not occur in the modern language — it existed historically but all such words have been replaced or the cluster simplified. Distributional gaps are evidence of history.
- **Let one or two consonants have two competing circumflex forms.** Maybe ⟨k⟩ before circumflex produces /k͜ʂʲ/ in careful or formal speech but just /kʲ/ in casual speech, and the "full" form is seen as old-fashioned. Or maybe the circumflex outcome for ⟨f⟩ is /p͜fʲ/ in some clans but /fʲ/ in others.
- **Note that some speakers don't use the circumflex at all for certain consonants.** Perhaps younger urban speakers are simplifying the system, treating the circumflex as just palatalization (/Cʲ/) for all consonants rather than producing the complex traditional outcomes. This is exactly what happens to complex morphophonological systems under language contact and modernization.

## 9. Disrupt the Numeral System

**The problem:** A clean sequence of 8 monomorphemic numerals for a base-8 system. Too tidy.

**What to do:**

- **Make "five" a compound or derived form.** In many natural numeral systems, the numbers above four or five show derivational complexity (Mandarin "eleven" = *shí-yī* "ten-one"; Ainu "six" = *i-wan* "4-from-10"). Maybe the Ferrestan word for 5, 6, or 7 is transparently derived from a combination of lower numbers or from a word for "hand" or "finger," partially eroded.
- **Have the system show its base more explicitly.** If 8 is the base, then 9 should be "eight-one" (= "ten-one" in their system). But show that this compound has been partially fused and reduced. Maybe "nine" (8+1) is *erfoji* but in casual speech it's *erji* — with the *fo* and *ji* blurring together. This shows a living system rather than a list.
- **Include a competing decimal loanword system** from contact with Mamkoslav or Bengali speakers, used for trade or in school contexts, coexisting with the native octal system. Bilingual numeral systems are common in contact situations (cf. Japanese using both native and Sino-Japanese numerals).

## 10. Make the Color System Less Complete

**The problem:** Eleven named color terms, perfectly covering the spectrum including two greens and two purples. This maps too neatly onto the Berlin-Kay hierarchy.

**What to do:**

- **Collapse or eliminate one or two terms.** Maybe *elam* (light purple/pink) and *kmid* (red) are used interchangeably by some speakers — the "pink" category is not firmly established as a separate term in some dialects. This is exactly how the Berlin-Kay hierarchy predicts color terms should be: languages at the boundary between Stage VI and VII have unstable pink/orange/gray/purple terms.
- **Make one "color" term actually be a texture or material term.** In many languages, what looks like a color term is actually a reference to a natural material. Perhaps *atma* (brown / earth color) is really just "earth-colored" and functions as a description rather than an abstract color category. Some speakers might not consider it a "real" color word.
- **Have the two green terms show dialectal distribution.** Perhaps *afcer* (green) is used everywhere, but *lafec* (dark green) is really only a distinction made by forest-dwelling inland clans who need it, while coastal speakers just use *afcer* for everything green.

## 11. Rough Up the Lexicon's Edges

**The problem:** The lexicon covers every standard typological category (body parts, kinship, colors, numerals, interrogatives, basic verbs, etc.) with suspiciously even coverage and no major gaps.

**What to do:**

- **Have a gap where you wouldn't expect one.** Maybe there is no native word for "left" (*rabnat*) — maybe the word is actually a taboo-avoidance term or a borrowing from Dzemali, and the "real" Ferrestan word fell out of use because left-handedness was inauspicious. (Many real languages have unstable or borrowed "left" words for exactly this reason — Latin *sinister* became a word for "evil.")
- **Have an area of unexpected richness that isn't universal.** The landscape terms are already rich, which is great. But add something that only this culture would need: maybe there are seven words for different kinds of mountain wind, or four words for different states of a cooking fire, or three words for different densities of fog at sea. Idiosyncratic hyperspecificity in one domain makes the rest of the lexicon feel more authentic by contrast.
- **Include a few words whose meaning is debated or unclear.** The entry *olw-* (listed with no definition) and *cs-* and *seuc-* (also undefined) are actually excellent — they feel like real undocumented items. Do this with some content words too. Maybe there is a word that the recorder heard repeatedly but could not determine the meaning of, or a word that different speakers defined differently.

---

## 12. Roughen the Example Texts

**The problem:** The two translated texts parse perfectly against the grammar. The untranslated poem is untranslated.

**What to do:**

- **Include a text where the grammar breaks its own rules.** In real-world field texts, informants produce forms that don't appear in the elicited paradigms. Maybe a story uses an article form that doesn't match the tables, and a footnote says "this form was consistently used in narratives but not in elicitation." This is incredibly common in fieldwork — performance grammar and elicited grammar never match perfectly.
- **Include a text with a loanword or code-switch.** A speaker telling a story about trade might drop in a Mamkoslav or Bengali word. This happens constantly in multilingual societies.
- **Have the untranslated poem contain a form that contradicts the grammar.** Perhaps one line uses what appears to be a verb without a particle — a bare root in a finite position. This would raise the question of whether the particle system is actually obligatory or just the dominant pattern, and whether there's an older "bare verb" construction preserved in poetry.

## Summary: The Principle Behind All of These

Every recommendation above follows from a single insight: **natural languages are the accumulated residue of thousands of small historical accidents, and the evidence of those accidents is what makes them feel real.**

A language feels constructed when every piece fits together according to a consistent logic. A language feels natural when some pieces fit perfectly, some fit awkwardly, some don't fit at all, and a few pieces are clearly borrowed from a different puzzle. The goal is not to make the language less systematic — it is to make it look like a systematic language that has been **battered by time, fractured by dialect, patched with borrowings, and used by real people who don't care about paradigm tables.**

The good news is that Ferrestan already has some of these features. The *arna*/*rana* dialectal variant, the *smaroktwa* opacity, the homophonous *kro* (three / rope), the unexplained *olw-*/*cs-*/*seuc-* prefixes, and the inconsistencies in the dajkê conjugation example (where the dative form appears as both *ajdkê* and *dajkê*) — whether intentional or accidental — are the most naturalistic parts of the language. The task is to extend that roughness into every system.
